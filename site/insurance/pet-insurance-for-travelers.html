Pet insurance for travelers (US): what policies cover away from home

If your dog or cat gets sick or hurt on a trip, will your US pet insurance pay? It depends on where you are, how long you’ve had the policy and what it excludes. Below: what to check, and what 11 US insurers say on their own websites about travel coverage, waiting periods, age limits and reimbursement.

Quick answer

  • US and Canada: most policies we checked reimburse care at any licensed vet in the US, and most also in Canada (some only during a temporary stay). Lemonade covers the US only.
  • Outside the US and Canada: only Embrace and Nationwide say their policies cover vet care abroad. The rest limit cover to the US (and usually Canada), sometimes adding US territories or military bases, or their sites don’t say (marked “Check” in the table).
  • Waiting periods: illness cover usually starts only after a waiting period of up to 30 days (14 or 15 days at most insurers we checked), so a policy bought the week before a trip may not cover that trip.
  • Pre-existing conditions are generally not covered, and that includes anything that first shows up during a waiting period.
  • You pay first: pet insurance usually reimburses you after you pay the vet, so budget for the full bill on the road.

Policies differ by state, product and underwriting company, and insurers change their terms. Confirm every detail with the insurer before you buy or travel. Last checked Oct 24, 2026.

Why insurance matters when you travel with a pet

Trips bring new risks: heat, unfamiliar food and water, other animals, car and airport stress. If your pet needs an emergency vet away from home, you usually don’t know the clinic, you normally pay the bill yourself first, and it can be large. Abroad, it may come in another currency and language.

Pet insurance doesn’t change what the vet charges, but it can pay back part of a covered bill afterwards. Separate travel insurance for people usually doesn’t cover pets (Trupanion notes this on its travel FAQ), so check your pet’s policy before you go.

Where coverage applies: US only, US and Canada, or abroad

  • US only: Lemonade. Care in Canada or abroad is not covered.
  • US and Canada: most insurers below. Some limit Canada to a temporary stay (Healthy Paws: “temporarily traveling”; Odie’s sample policy: 90 days or less). Trupanion and Pets Best also name US territories and US military bases abroad.
  • Abroad: Embrace and Nationwide say coverage applies outside the US and Canada. Embrace says up to 6 consecutive months per trip; Nationwide says any licensed vet anywhere. Confirm the exact policy wording before an international trip.

Waiting periods and pre-existing conditions

A waiting period is the time after the policy starts before claims are paid. Anything that starts during it is usually treated as pre-existing and never covered. Among the insurers below, accident waiting periods range from none to 15 days, and illness waiting periods from 14 days or less to 30 days. Orthopedic or knee (cruciate ligament) conditions can wait up to 6 months, or 12 months for hip dysplasia at Healthy Paws. Some insurers shorten these after a vet exam. If you’re planning a trip, compare start dates early.

Pre-existing conditions are excluded. Some insurers will cover a curable condition again once it has been cured and symptom-free for a set time (Spot: 180 days; Lemonade, in most states: 12 months), but not knee or ligament conditions.

Before you travel: a short checklist

  • Read the territory clause in your policy (often called “Territory” or “Treatment locations”) for every country on your route.
  • Check that your waiting periods will be over before you leave.
  • Save your policy number and the insurer’s claims phone number or app on your phone.
  • Carry your pet’s medical and vaccination records. Insurers usually ask for them with a claim.
  • Keep itemized invoices and payment receipts from any vet you visit; abroad, ask for an English version if possible.
  • Look up an emergency vet near where you’ll stay.
