K9 · Working dogs

Traveling with working dogs and retired K9s

Last checked: November 2026

This page is for handlers and adopters travelling with a military working dog, a police or law-enforcement dog, a detection or search-and-rescue dog, another official working or K9 dog, or a retired K9 coming home after adoption.

A working dog is not automatically a service animal. US airline rules for official-duty K9s are separate from the US Department of Transportation (DOT) service-animal rule, and they are not the same from one airline to the next. A retired military working dog usually travels as a pet unless it independently qualifies as a service animal.

Everything below is an official rule published by a government, a regulator or the airline itself. There is no field-verified report on this page. Confirm the current text with the airline or authority before you travel.

Veterans travelling with a psychiatric or other service dog should also read Service dogs and veterans. Handlers on a permanent change of station can use Military PCS with pets.

Working dog, service animal or pet?

DOT service animals (14 CFR Part 382). On flights to, from or within the United States, a service animal is a dog, of any breed or type, that is individually trained to do work or perform tasks for a person with a disability, including a psychiatric disability. Emotional support animals, comfort animals, companionship animals and service animals in training are not service animals. Airlines may treat those animals as pets. The only forms an airline may require for a service animal are DOT’s forms: the DOT Service Animal Air Transportation Form and, on flights of 8 hours or more, the relief attestation. See DOT — Service Animals.

Working, police and detection dogs. A dog that works for a military unit, a law-enforcement agency or a detection or rescue team is not a service animal under the DOT rule unless it is also individually trained to perform tasks for a person with a disability. If it does not meet that definition, it travels either under the airline’s working-dog or official-duty provision, if the airline has one and you qualify, or as a pet.

Retired K9s. After adoption, a retired military working dog is no longer travelling on official duty. Unless the dog qualifies as your service animal under the DOT definition, the airline’s pet rules apply, including cabin size limits, fees and cargo or embargo rules.

US airline rules

The eight US airlines below each publish their own working-dog or official-duty language. Several accept only some kinds of working dogs, or only some handlers. A large Belgian Malinois, German Shepherd or Labrador often cannot fit under a seat; that matters most on airlines that do not carry animals in the hold.

Compare the full pet pages at US airlines and the airline comparison.

American Airlines

Military and police dogs working in detection, security enforcement and combat must be listed when you book. American waives pet charges for trained active military dogs travelling in the cabin if they are on official duty and have written documents. Working dogs trained to attack are not permitted in the cabin and must be checked. American accepts checked pets at the ticket counter for active-duty US military travelling on assignment; fees and restrictions apply.

Source: American Airlines — Military travel benefits. See also American Airlines pet policy.

Delta Air Lines

Delta’s contract of carriage (Rule 9) accepts, without charge, dogs trained in explosive detection or search and rescue only when they are accompanied by US military or US government handlers. The dog may go into the cabin but may not occupy a seat. A state or local police handler is not covered by that sentence.

Separately, Delta accepts fully trained service dogs that assist a person with a disability. Emotional support animals are not service animals. Submit the DOT Service Animal Air Transportation Form at least 48 hours before departure.

Sources: Delta — Contract of Carriage, Delta Domestic General Rules Tariff (modified 7 July 2026), Delta — Trained service animals. See also Delta pet policy.

United Airlines

United’s contract of carriage (revised 30 September 2026), Rule 16, accepts without charge a properly harnessed dog trained in explosive detection, drug search, and rescue, or other specific functions, when the handler is on official emergency business authorised by a federal, state or local government agency. That official-duty status must be documented in writing to United’s satisfaction. The dog may accompany the handler in the cabin but may not occupy a seat.

United’s service-animal definition still follows DOT for dogs trained to assist a person with a disability. A passenger with a service animal (other than a search-and-rescue animal) must submit United’s Service Animal Air Transportation Form at least 48 hours before departure when the ticket was bought more than 48 hours ahead.

Source: United — Contract of Carriage, Rule 16. See also United pet policy.

Alaska Airlines and Hawaiian Airlines

Alaska and Hawaiian share one contract of carriage (revised 25 August 2026). Rule 14.D accepts without charge a properly harnessed dog trained in law enforcement, explosive detection, drug search, and search and rescue, or other specific functions, when the handler is on official business authorised by a federal, state or local government agency. Official-duty status must be documented in writing. The passenger must present a letter of mission and a copy of the dog’s certification. The dog may go into the cabin but may not occupy a seat. Alaska may refuse an animal that appears incompatible with air travel.

Pets that are not travelling under that official-duty rule may go in the cabin if they fit in an under-seat carrier, or in the climate-controlled baggage compartment. Baggage-compartment travel is subject to breed restrictions, date embargoes and fleet limits. Published pet fees range from 100 to 200 US dollars each way, per kennel, on Alaska or Hawaiian operated flights. Trained service animals travel free.

Sources: Alaska Airlines — Contract of Carriage, Traveling with pets — Alaska Airlines and Hawaiian Airlines. See also Alaska pet policy and Hawaiian pet policy.

Southwest Airlines

Southwest accepts fully trained law-enforcement dogs trained in explosives or drug detection (or other specific functions) and search-and-rescue dogs, without charge, when they travel with their handlers on official business. The handler must present a letter of mission and a copy of the animal’s certification. The dog may not occupy a seat or sit in an emergency exit row, and must not block an evacuation path. Dogs in training are not accepted. Southwest will not carry law-enforcement, search-and-rescue or military working dogs in the cargo compartment.

Military working dogs travel as part of a military working dog team (a trained military handler and a trained canine). Southwest states that it has no restrictions on the primary breeds used in the military working dog program: Belgian Malinois, Dutch Shepherd, Labrador and German Shepherd. Book through the Defense Travel System, then confirm the reservation with Southwest Customer Service or the government hotline (877-633-4425) so that the dog’s name and tattoo number appear in the reservation notes. Refer to the canine only as a military working dog, not as a service animal. At check-in the handler presents a Department of Defense ID and a DD Form 1610 or permanent-change-of-station order that includes the dog’s name and tattoo number. The handler may buy a second seat for extra floor space; the dog still may not occupy the seat. A boxed muzzle and kennel labels such as “Danger” or “Caution” are treated as Department of Defense uniform requirements, not as a behaviour warning. Kennels and other team equipment travel as checked baggage or cargo.

Sources: Southwest — Military travel and benefits, Southwest Contract of Carriage (59th revision, effective 6 November 2026). See also Southwest pet policy.

JetBlue

JetBlue accepts only search-and-rescue or explosive-detection canines. It does not accept private-organisation, patrol (bitework), dual-purpose or drug-detection canines. Travel must be domestic within the United States, in Main. One working canine is allowed per handler and two per flight. The handler must present official documentation on US military, government-agency or law-enforcement letterhead covering the canine’s qualifications and the reason for travel. There is no additional fee for an approved working canine.

Source: JetBlue — Law Enforcement Officers. See also JetBlue pet policy.

Frontier Airlines

Frontier’s contract of carriage (R0, 16 January 2026) accepts, without charge and in the cabin, animals trained in explosive detection, contraband search or search and rescue that are on active duty and travelling for that purpose. The passenger must present credible documentation that the animal is travelling for that purpose. The animal must fit under the seat or on the passenger’s lap (not on the lap in row 1), may not occupy a seat, and may not sit in an emergency exit row or block an aisle. An animal that cannot meet those seating rules will not be accepted.

Frontier’s pet FAQ (last modified 17 September 2026) states that household pets travel in the cabin on US domestic flights and that Frontier does not transport pets in the cargo hold. Checked pets are not allowed. Only service dogs are allowed on international flights, and they must meet CDC requirements.

Sources: Frontier — Contract of Carriage (English PDF), Frontier — Do you allow pets on the plane?. See also Frontier pet policy.

Large working breeds and crates

Belgian Malinois, German Shepherds and Labradors are the primary breeds Southwest lists for the military working dog program, and they are typical working-dog sizes. Unless an airline accepts the dog in the cabin under an official-duty rule, a dog of that size usually cannot travel as a cabin pet.

  • Southwest carries the dog in the cabin and the kennel as checked baggage or cargo. It does not put the military working dog itself in the cargo compartment.
  • Frontier requires any accepted working dog to fit under the seat or on the lap, and it does not carry pets in the hold, so a large working breed may not be able to travel on Frontier.
  • Alaska and Hawaiian accept pets in a climate-controlled baggage compartment when space and the published breed, embargo and fleet rules allow it. They do not transfer a baggage-compartment pet to another carrier; the pet must be claimed and rechecked.
  • American accepts checked pets for active-duty US military travelling on assignment; attack-trained working dogs must be checked rather than ride in the cabin.
  • JetBlue’s working-canine rule is a cabin rule on domestic Main flights.

If the dog will travel in a kennel, use one that meets the airline’s published size and construction rules. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency tells travellers to find an appropriately sized crate and points to the International Air Transport Association guide for pet travel by air. See our guide to IATA hard crates for cargo and confirm the airline’s current kennel limits before you buy or ship a crate.

US federal rules

DOT service animals

See DOT — Service Animals and the DOT Service Animal Air Transportation Form. The DOT rule decides who must be accepted as a service animal. It does not, by itself, create a right for a police, detection or military working dog to fly in the cabin.

CDC dog import rules (since 1 August 2024)

Dogs entering the United States, including dogs coming home, must generally:

  • have a receipt for the CDC Dog Import Form;
  • be at least 6 months old;
  • have a microchip;
  • appear healthy on arrival.

Extra documents apply if the dog has been in a country that CDC lists as high-risk for dog rabies during the past 6 months. Dogs arriving from rabies-free or low-risk countries follow the lower-document path when they have spent the past 6 months only in those countries. Confirm the current list and form with CDC before travel. The CDC pages describe these steps for dogs entering the United States; they do not publish a separate working-dog waiver.

USDA APHIS export and health certificates

The destination country sets the entry rules. A USDA-accredited veterinarian prepares the export health certificate and submits it for APHIS endorsement when endorsement is required. Tell the veterinarian about transit stops, whether the dog will travel in the cabin or as cargo, and the departure date. Certificates are created and submitted through VEHCS. See APHIS — Take a pet from the United States to another country.

For dogs going to Mexico, APHIS requires a New World screwworm inspection certificate signed by a USDA-accredited veterinarian within 5 days before entry (in force since 3 August 2026). APHIS endorsement is not required for that form. See APHIS — Pet travel from the US to Mexico.

Retired military working dogs: adoption and transport

Two different sections of title 10 of the US Code apply.

10 U.S.C. § 2583 covers transfer and adoption of military animals, including military working dogs:

  • The Secretary of the military department concerned must make a suitable military animal available for transfer or adoption at the end of its useful life, when it is otherwise excess, or earlier in unusual or extraordinary circumstances, including when the handler is killed in action, dies of wounds, or is medically retired from injuries received in action.
  • Recommended priority is adoption by former handlers, then by other persons or organisations able to care for the animal humanely, then transfer to law-enforcement agencies.
  • If the handler was wounded in action, the dog is made available for adoption only by that handler. If the handler was killed in action or died of wounds, the dog is made available only to a parent, child, spouse or sibling of the deceased handler.
  • The transfer is without charge to the recipient.
  • When a military working dog is to be retired, the Secretary transports the dog to the Veterinary Treatment Facility at Lackland Air Force Base, Texas, for a full veterinary screening and necessary care, then transports the dog to the 341st Training Squadron or another location in the United States for transfer or adoption. If the dog was outside the United States immediately before that screening and a US citizen or member of the armed forces living abroad agrees to adopt it, the dog may be transported to that location instead.
  • Transportation to the United States is not required if the dog is located outside the United States and the Secretary determines that moving it would not be in the dog’s medical best interests.
  • Former handlers receive a preference in the adoption of a retired military working dog, unless adoption by the former handler would not be in the best interests of the dog.

10 U.S.C. § 994 does not cover transport or adoption. It allows the Secretary of Defense to establish a system of veterinary care for military working dogs adopted under section 2583, and it states that no funds may be provided by the federal government for that purpose.

After adoption, bringing the dog home on a commercial flight follows the airline’s pet rules and any destination country’s entry rules, unless the dog independently qualifies as a service animal. See Military PCS with pets if you are moving under orders.

Country rules

Working, police and military dogs still have to meet the destination’s animal-health rules unless that authority publishes a specific exemption. Where an official exemption exists, it is usually about the point of entry or paperwork channel, not a waiver of rabies vaccination or identification.

European Union

Since 22 April 2026, the animal-health rules for non-commercial movements of pet dogs, cats and ferrets are in Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/131, with certificate and passport models in Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/705 and the listed-country lists in Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/636. They replace Regulations (EU) 576/2013 and 577/2013.

The European Commission guide to bringing a pet into the EU from a non-EU country restates those rules: the dog travels with its owner or an authorised person; a maximum of five dogs, cats or ferrets in a single vehicle; an ISO microchip; rabies vaccination at 12 weeks or older, then a 21-day wait after the primary course; a rabies antibody titre test unless the country is listed; and an EU animal-health certificate (Annex III to 2026/705). The United States, Canada, Mexico, the United Kingdom and Japan appear on the Commission list of territories and non-EU countries. South Korea does not. The Commission guide does not describe a separate health-rule waiver for working or police dogs. Ask the competent authority of the Member State of entry if you need a special arrangement for an official working dog.

See also France, Germany and the pet travel checklist.

United Kingdom (Great Britain)

Bring a pet dog, cat or ferret to Great Britain sets the usual entry steps: microchip, rabies vaccination with a 21-day wait after the primary vaccination, the correct travel document, an approved route and carrier, and a non-commercial declaration. Dogs also need tapeworm treatment unless they arrive directly from a listed exemption country. Ordinary pets cannot travel in the cabin on commercial flights into Great Britain.

Assimilated Regulation (EU) 576/2013, Article 10(3), still provides a specific entry-point rule for Great Britain: the authority may authorise registered military or search-and-rescue dogs to enter through a point other than a travellers’ point of entry if the owner or authorised person applies in advance for a permit and the dogs undergo compliance checks at a place designated in that permit. That is a point-of-entry arrangement, not a waiver of identification, rabies or documentation rules. See legislation.gov.uk — Regulation 576/2013, Article 10.

If the dog is being sold, transferred or rehomed, or cannot travel within five days of its owner, Great Britain treats the movement as commercial (Balai). That matters when a retired K9 is adopted and then moved. See Commercial import of dogs, cats and ferrets (IIN BLLV/5b) and contact APHA at imports@apha.gov.uk.

The United States is a listed country for Great Britain pet travel. South Korea is not; a dog coming from an unlisted country needs a rabies titre test and a three-month wait after the blood sample. See the UK country guide.

Canada

The Canadian Food Inspection Agency page Travelling with your dog states that import requirements depend on the country of origin, the dog’s age, the purpose of the import, and whether the dog is travelling with its owner. Check the requirements for your situation on that page before you book. The page does not publish a separate exemption for police, military or detection dogs. If you are adopting or buying a dog from another country, the same import requirements apply. For export from Canada, CFIA describes an export certificate issued by a licensed veterinarian and endorsed by an official CFIA veterinarian.

See the Canada country guide.

Mexico

Dogs entering Mexico are presented at a SENASICA OISA inspection office. For dogs travelling from the United States, APHIS requires a New World screwworm inspection certificate signed by a USDA-accredited veterinarian within 5 days before entry (since 3 August 2026), without APHIS endorsement. After inspection, SENASICA issues the import animal-health certificate (CZI). Dirty bedding, toys and extra food may be removed; only that day’s food ration is allowed.

Dogs returning from Mexico to the United States need a screwworm-freedom certificate from a SENASICA-approved veterinarian, with the inspection no more than 5 days before travel, plus the CDC Dog Import Form, a minimum age of 6 months, a microchip and a healthy appearance. Mexico is not on CDC’s high-risk dog-rabies list; the screwworm rules are separate. Use SENASICA’s directory of approved clinic veterinarians (MVRA). Agency information: SENASICA. Detailed US-to-Mexico steps: APHIS — Pet travel from the US to Mexico.

No official SENASICA exemption for working, police or military dogs was published on the pages reviewed for this guide. See the Mexico country guide.

Japan

Japan’s Animal Quarantine Service treats the US mainland (other than Hawaii and Guam) as a non-designated region. Import from a non-designated region requires an ISO microchip before the first rabies vaccination, a two-dose rabies series, a rabies titre of at least 0.5 IU/ml, a 180-day wait from the blood draw, advance notice to AQS at least 40 days ahead, a clinical examination within 10 days before boarding, and a government-endorsed certificate (Form AC). See MAFF AQS — Importation of dogs and cats from non-designated regions and the USA to Japan route guide. The AQS pages reviewed do not publish a separate working-dog waiver.

South Korea

Dogs 90 days or older arriving from a country that is not classified as rabies-free need an ISO 11784/11785 microchip, a rabies-neutralising antibody result of 0.5 IU/ml or more from an approved laboratory, and a government health certificate that states the microchip number and the titre result. See APQA — Bring a dog or a cat to Korea and the USA to South Korea route guide. South Korea is not on the EU listed-country list or on Great Britain’s listed-country table, so a dog leaving Korea for the EU or Great Britain needs the applicable rabies titre and waiting period. The APQA page reviewed does not publish a separate working-dog waiver.

Practical checklist

  1. Decide how the dog is travelling: official-duty working dog, DOT service animal, or pet (including most retired K9s).
  2. Read the operating airline’s current page and contract of carriage. Call or write in advance if you will use an official-duty cabin rule, and have the reservation note the dog correctly (Southwest asks handlers not to call a military working dog a service animal).
  3. If the dog is a service animal for a person with a disability, complete the DOT Service Animal Air Transportation Form the way the airline asks you to, usually at least 48 hours ahead.
  4. If the dog will enter the United States, complete the CDC Dog Import Form and meet the age, microchip and health rules, plus any high-risk-country documents.
  5. If the dog will leave the United States, ask a USDA-accredited veterinarian about the destination certificate and VEHCS endorsement. Mention cabin versus cargo and any transit stops.
  6. Check the destination authority: EU Commission guide, UK APHA / GOV.UK, CFIA, SENASICA and APHIS Mexico, Japan AQS, Korea APQA.
  7. If you are adopting a retired military working dog, read 10 U.S.C. § 2583. After adoption, follow pet or service-animal rules, not the official-duty working-dog rule, unless you are still travelling on official orders.
  8. If the dog will travel in a kennel, confirm the airline’s crate rules and see IATA hard crates for cargo.
  9. Use the pet travel checklist and, where you need a document pack, the passport page.

Frequently asked questions

Is a police or detection dog a service animal on a US flight?

Not automatically. Under the DOT rule, a service animal is a dog trained to perform tasks for a person with a disability. A working, police or detection dog travels under the airline’s official-duty working-dog rule if the airline has one and you qualify, or as a pet.

Can a retired military working dog fly as a service animal?

Only if that individual dog is trained to do work or perform tasks for a person with a disability and you complete the airline’s DOT-form process. Adoption under 10 U.S.C. § 2583 does not, by itself, make the dog a service animal.

Which of these airlines accept official working dogs in the cabin?

American (trained active military dogs on official duty; attack-trained dogs must be checked), Delta (explosive-detection or search-and-rescue dogs with US military or US government handlers), United (official emergency government business), Alaska and Hawaiian (official government business, with a letter of mission and certification), Southwest (official-business law-enforcement and search-and-rescue dogs, and military working dog teams), JetBlue (search-and-rescue or explosive-detection canines on domestic Main flights) and Frontier (explosive detection, contraband search or search and rescue on active duty, if the dog fits under the seat or on the lap). Always read the airline’s current page.

Do I still need the CDC Dog Import Form for a working dog entering the United States?

CDC requires the Dog Import Form, a minimum age of 6 months, a microchip and a healthy appearance for dogs entering the United States, with extra documents after time in a high-risk country. CDC’s published pages do not describe a separate working-dog waiver. Confirm with CDC before travel.

How does adoption of a retired military working dog work?

Federal law (10 U.S.C. § 2583) requires suitable military working dogs to be made available for adoption or transfer, gives former handlers priority, and sets out transport to the veterinary facility at Lackland Air Force Base and then to the 341st Training Squadron or another US location, or to an overseas adopter who is a US citizen or service member. The US government does not charge the recipient for the transfer. Section 994 allows a veterinary-care system for adopted dogs but provides that no federal funds may be used for it.

If I adopt a retired K9 abroad and bring it to Great Britain, is that pet travel?

Not if ownership is changing or the dog is being rehomed. Great Britain treats sale, transfer or rehoming as a commercial (Balai) movement. Contact APHA before you travel. Registered military or search-and-rescue dogs may be authorised to use a non-travellers’ point of entry with an advance permit; that permit does not replace health documents.

Can a large Malinois or German Shepherd fly in the cabin?

Only if the airline’s official-duty rule accepts that dog and the dog can be accommodated in the cabin under the airline’s seating and safety rules. Frontier requires the animal to fit under the seat or on the passenger’s lap. Southwest states that it does not restrict the primary military working dog breeds and that those dogs travel in the cabin as part of a military working dog team. If no official-duty cabin rule applies, a large dog is subject to the airline’s pet and cargo rules, which on some airlines means it cannot travel at all.
