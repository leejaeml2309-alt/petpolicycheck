Traveling with your pet: what your vet needs to prepare

Last checked: November 2026

This page is for clinic teams and owners planning an international trip with a dog or cat. It lists the veterinary steps in the order they usually have to happen, and it points to official government pages and to PetPolicyCheck’s country and route guides.

The destination sets the entry rules. A USDA-accredited veterinarian prepares the export health certificate for a pet leaving the United States and submits it for endorsement when endorsement is required. Tell the veterinarian about transit stops, whether the pet will travel in the cabin or as cargo, and the departure date. See APHIS — Take a pet from the United States to another country.

Everything below is an official rule published by a government or regulator. Confirm the current text with the destination authority and with APHIS or the equivalent official body before you book or issue a certificate.

A printable clinic checklist is included below, and as a separate print-friendly A4 sheet. Owners can also use the pet travel checklist, the Pet Travel Passport and the rabies titer countdown.

The veterinary steps, in order

1. Microchip

Identify the pet with a microchip before any required rabies vaccination, if the destination says the chip must come first.

  • The European Commission requires an ISO microchip for dogs, cats and ferrets entering the EU from a non-EU country.
  • Japan’s Animal Quarantine Service requires an ISO microchip, implanted before the first rabies vaccination, for imports from non-designated regions (the US mainland other than Hawaii and Guam).
  • South Korea’s APQA requires an ISO 11784/11785 microchip for dogs and cats, including animals under 90 days old.
  • CDC requires a microchip for dogs entering the United States.
  • GOV.UK requires a microchip for pets entering Great Britain.

Record the chip number exactly as it will appear on the rabies certificate, the titer report and the export health certificate. If the scanner cannot read the chip, destination checks will fail even when the paperwork looks complete.

2. Rabies vaccination

Vaccinate after the microchip is in place whenever the destination requires that order.

  • European Union: rabies vaccination at 12 weeks of age or older, then a 21-day wait after the primary course before travel. See the Commission guide.
  • Great Britain: rabies vaccination and a 21-day wait after the primary vaccination. See Bring a pet to Great Britain.
  • Japan (non-designated regions, including the US mainland except Hawaii and Guam): first rabies vaccination when the pet is at least 91 days old; second vaccination at least 30 days later. See MAFF AQS.
  • United States entry: dogs must be at least 6 months old. See CDC — Bringing a dog into the United States.

Keep the manufacturer, batch number, date of vaccination and date of validity on the record that will travel with the certificate.

3. Rabies titer test, if the destination requires one

A neutralizing-antibody titer is not required for every destination. It is required when the official list or laboratory rule says so.

  • European Union: a rabies antibody titration test is required unless the country of dispatch is listed in Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/636. The United States, Canada, Mexico, the United Kingdom and Japan are listed. South Korea is not listed. See the Commission listing of territories and non-EU countries. For an unlisted country, the Commission guide states that the blood sample is taken at least 30 days after the primary vaccination and not less than 90 days before the certificate is issued.
  • Great Britain: pets from unlisted countries need a rabies blood test, then a 3-month wait after the blood sample, before entry. The United States is listed. South Korea is not. See GOV.UK — rabies blood tests and which pet travel document.
  • Japan (non-designated regions): a titer of at least 0.5 IU/ml, then a 180-day wait counted from the date the blood was drawn. See MAFF AQS. Use the titer countdown to plan that wait.
  • South Korea: dogs and cats 90 days or older arriving from a country that is not classified as rabies-free need a rabies-neutralising antibody result of 0.5 IU/ml or more from an approved laboratory, and a government health certificate that states the microchip number and the titer result. See APQA.
  • Entering the United States from a high-risk country: extra CDC documents apply if the dog has been in a high-risk country for dog rabies during the past 6 months. For a dog that was vaccinated in the United States, the Certification of U.S.-Issued Rabies Vaccination must be endorsed before the dog leaves the United States; it cannot be issued retroactively.

Dogs that have spent the past 6 months only in rabies-free or low-risk countries follow CDC’s lower-document path: the Dog Import Form, age, microchip and a healthy appearance.

4. Health certificate and clinical examination

The destination names the certificate model. The examining veterinarian must see the pet while that model is still valid.

  • European Union: the non-commercial animal health certificate is Annex III to Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/705. The Commission guide states that it is valid for 10 days until the EU entry check (plus the duration of a sea journey) and then covers onward EU travel for 6 months or until the rabies vaccination expires, whichever is earlier. APHIS requires the new EU non-commercial certificates from 1 October 2026. See APHIS — pet travel from the US to France and to Germany.
  • European Union (tapeworm, dogs): dogs entering Finland, Ireland, Malta, Northern Ireland or Norway must be treated against Echinococcus multilocularis by a veterinarian not more than 120 hours and not less than 24 hours before scheduled entry. See the Commission guide.
  • Great Britain: a Great Britain pet health certificate, issued by a USDA-accredited veterinarian. Enter Great Britain within 10 days of the certificate (APHIS: within 10 days of APHIS endorsement). See GOV.UK — Great Britain pet health certificate and APHIS — pet travel from the US to the United Kingdom. Dogs also need praziquantel (or an equivalent proven against Echinococcus multilocularis) no less than 24 hours and no more than 120 hours before arrival, unless they come directly from Finland, Ireland, Northern Ireland, Malta or Norway. See GOV.UK — tapeworm treatment for dogs.
  • Japan: a clinical examination within 10 days before boarding, advance notice to AQS at least 40 days ahead, and government-endorsed Form AC (or the alternative APHIS accepts). See MAFF AQS and APHIS — pet travel from the US to Japan.
  • South Korea: a government health certificate stating the microchip number and, when a titer is required, the titer result. APHIS: a USDA-accredited veterinarian issues the certificate through VEHCS; APHIS ink-signs and embosses it; the original travels with the pet. See APQA and APHIS — pet travel from the US to Korea.
  • Mexico (dogs from the United States): a New World screwworm inspection certificate signed by a USDA-accredited veterinarian within 5 days before entry (in force since 3 August 2026). APHIS endorsement is not required for that form. SENASICA inspects the dog at an OISA office and then issues the import certificate (CZI). See APHIS — pet travel from the US to Mexico. Dogs returning from Mexico to the United States need a screwworm-freedom certificate from a SENASICA-approved veterinarian, with the inspection no more than 5 days before travel, plus the CDC Dog Import Form.
  • Canada: import requirements depend on the country of origin, the animal’s age, the purpose of the import and whether the animal is travelling with its owner. Check CFIA — Travelling with your dog and APHIS — pet travel from the US to Canada for the model that applies to your patient.

5. Endorsement by APHIS (VEHCS) or the official authority

For pets leaving the United States, a USDA-accredited veterinarian creates and submits the certificate in VEHCS. Electronic signatures are accepted from accredited veterinarians for issuance. Whether APHIS then endorses the certificate in ink or digitally still depends on the destination:

  • United Kingdom, France, Germany and Korea: APHIS ink-signs and embosses; the original paper certificate travels with the pet.
  • Japan: APHIS digitally endorses dog and cat certificates; carry a printed copy.
  • Mexico (New World screwworm dog form since 3 August 2026): no APHIS endorsement.

APHIS publishes endorsement fees separately; check that page for the current amount.

If the pet is leaving another country, the official veterinary authority of that country endorses the certificate. In Canada, CFIA describes an export certificate issued by a licensed veterinarian and then endorsed by an official CFIA veterinarian. See CFIA — Travelling with your dog.

Printable checklist

Print this list, or open the A4 printable checklist.

  • Destination confirmed and official rules opened (country or route page above)
  • Microchip implanted and readable; number copied onto every document
  • Rabies vaccination given in the order the destination requires, with dates and validity recorded
  • 21-day wait after a primary rabies course, if travelling to the EU or Great Britain
  • Titer arranged, if required (EU unlisted countries; Great Britain unlisted countries; Japan non-designated regions; South Korea from a non-rabies-free origin)
  • Waiting period after the blood draw completed (EU 90-day rule; Great Britain 3 months; Japan 180 days)
  • Destination certificate model prepared in VEHCS or the equivalent official system
  • Clinical examination done inside the certificate window (EU and Great Britain: 10 days; Japan: examination within 10 days before boarding)
  • APHIS or other official endorsement completed, unless the destination says none is needed (Mexico screwworm dog form)
  • Original or printed endorsed certificate packed to travel with the pet
  • CDC Dog Import Form receipt, if the dog will enter the United States
  • High-risk-country documents, if they apply, including any US rabies certification endorsed before departure
  • Great Britain tapeworm treatment recorded, if the dog is going to GB
  • EU tapeworm treatment recorded, if the dog is going to Finland, Ireland, Malta, Northern Ireland or Norway
  • Japan AQS notified at least 40 days ahead, if going to Japan
  • Airline told about cabin versus cargo; crate or carrier confirmed

Frequently asked questions

Does every destination need a rabies titer?

No. The European Union waives the titer for countries listed in Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/636, which include the United States, Canada, Mexico, the United Kingdom and Japan. Great Britain also uses a listed-country table; the United States is listed and South Korea is not. Japan requires a titer and a 180-day wait for pets coming from non-designated regions. South Korea requires a titer of at least 0.5 IU/ml for dogs and cats 90 days or older from a country that is not rabies-free.

When should we start for Japan?

For a pet coming from a non-designated region, plan from the longest official waits: an ISO microchip before the first rabies vaccine, a two-dose rabies series (first dose at 91 days of age or older, second dose at least 30 days later), a titer of at least 0.5 IU/ml, a 180-day wait from the blood draw, notice to AQS at least 40 days ahead, and a clinical examination within 10 days before boarding.

Can an owner complete the APHIS endorsement?

No. For pets leaving the United States, a USDA-accredited veterinarian prepares the certificate and submits it through VEHCS. APHIS endorses it when the destination requires endorsement. See VEHCS.

Do cats use the CDC Dog Import Form?

No. APHIS states that cats entering the United States do not use the CDC Dog Import Form. They must appear healthy; state, airline, Hawaii and Guam rules can still apply.
