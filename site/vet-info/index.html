Vet info for traveling with your pet

Last checked: November 2026

This page is the starting point for the health paperwork most destinations ask a veterinarian to prepare: a microchip, a rabies vaccination, a rabies titer test when one is required, and a health certificate that an official authority may need to endorse.

The destination sets the entry rules. For pets leaving the United States, a USDA-accredited veterinarian prepares the export certificate and submits it for endorsement when endorsement is required. Tell the veterinarian about transit stops, whether the pet will travel in the cabin or as cargo, and the departure date. See APHIS — Take a pet from the United States to another country.

The ordered clinic protocol is on what your vet needs to prepare. Country, map and route pages below give the precise rules for each destination. Confirm the current text with the destination authority and with APHIS, or the equivalent official body, before you issue a certificate or travel.

Microchip

Most destinations require the pet to be identified by a microchip, and many require the chip to be in place before the rabies vaccination if that order is part of the rule.

  • The European Commission requires an ISO microchip for dogs, cats and ferrets entering the EU from a non-EU country.
  • Japan’s Animal Quarantine Service requires an ISO microchip, implanted before the first rabies vaccination, for imports from non-designated regions.
  • South Korea’s APQA requires an ISO 11784/11785 microchip.
  • CDC requires a microchip for dogs entering the United States.
  • GOV.UK requires a microchip for pets entering Great Britain.

The number on the scanner must match every later document: the rabies certificate, any titer report and the export health certificate.

Rabies vaccination

Vaccinate after the microchip whenever the destination requires that order.

  • European Union: vaccination at 12 weeks of age or older, then a 21-day wait after the primary course. See the Commission guide.
  • Great Britain: rabies vaccination and a 21-day wait after the primary vaccination. See Bring a pet to Great Britain.
  • Entering the United States: dogs must be at least 6 months old. See CDC — Bringing a dog into the United States. Cats entering the United States do not use the CDC Dog Import Form; they must appear healthy, and state, airline, Hawaii and Guam rules can still apply. See APHIS — Cats.

Other destinations set their own age and dose schedule. Open the country or route page for that trip rather than treating the list above as complete.

Rabies titer test

A neutralizing-antibody titer is not required for every destination. It is required when the official list or laboratory rule says so.

  • The European Union waives the test for countries listed in Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/636, including the United States, Canada, Mexico, the United Kingdom and Japan. South Korea is not listed. See the Commission listing. For an unlisted country, the Commission guide states that the blood sample is taken at least 30 days after the primary vaccination and not less than 90 days before the certificate is issued.
  • Great Britain uses a listed-country table to decide whether a blood test and a 3-month wait after the sample are required. See GOV.UK — which pet travel document and rabies blood tests.
  • Japan requires a result of at least 0.5 IU/ml and a 180-day wait from the blood draw for pets coming from non-designated regions. See MAFF AQS and the titer countdown.
  • South Korea requires a result of at least 0.5 IU/ml for dogs and cats 90 days or older arriving from a country that is not classified as rabies-free. See APQA.

Health certificate and endorsement

The destination names the certificate model. The examining veterinarian must see the pet while that model is still valid.

For pets leaving the United States, a USDA-accredited veterinarian creates and submits the certificate in VEHCS. Whether APHIS then endorses it in ink or digitally depends on the destination; some certificates travel as an original paper document, and some destinations do not require APHIS endorsement at all.

If the pet is leaving another country, that country’s official veterinary authority endorses the certificate. In Canada, import rules depend on origin, age, purpose and whether the animal travels with its owner; see CFIA — Travelling with your dog.

Typical validity windows published by official sources include 10 days to an EU entry check, 10 days to enter Great Britain, and a clinical examination within 10 days before boarding for Japan. See the Commission guide, the Great Britain pet health certificate and MAFF AQS. Use the country or route page for the model that applies to your patient.

Some destinations also require parasite treatment shortly before arrival. Dogs entering Great Britain, or entering Finland, Ireland, Malta, Northern Ireland or Norway from outside the EU, need veterinarian-administered treatment against Echinococcus multilocularis in a 24-to-120-hour window. See GOV.UK — tapeworm treatment for dogs and the Commission guide.

Preparation at the vet

Book a USDA-accredited veterinarian (or the official equivalent outside the United States) early enough for any titer waiting period. Bring the pet’s current records and the exact destination, transit points, travel date and cabin-versus-cargo plan. APHIS asks owners to share those details so the certificate matches the journey.
