United States legal guide

Last checked: 19 November 2026

Please recheck the official pages before traveling. These rules change. This page is a plain-language summary, not legal advice and not a substitute for the official text. Official rules were checked on 19 November 2026 (UTC+9).

Before you start

Adopting a dog or cat — at home in the United States, or from another country — is a wonderful thing. It also comes with official health and entry rules. Those rules exist to keep people and animals safe, especially from rabies and other animal diseases.

This page covers:

  • Official U.S. rules for bringing an adopted dog or cat into the United States from another country
  • The difference between an individual adopter bringing their own pet and a rescue organization importing dogs for adoption
  • A dedicated Korea to USA corridor
  • The official basics of moving a dog or cat already in the United States from one state or territory to another

It does not cover airline policies (ask the airline), the rules for leaving the country of origin (for example, South Korea’s own export rules), or a list of shelters. Those may be added later.

Two U.S. agencies matter most for dogs:

  • The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) sets rabies and public-health entry rules for every dog entering or returning to the United States.
  • The U.S. Department of Agriculture, Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service (USDA APHIS) sets extra rules for dogs imported for resale, including adoption for a fee or donation, and additional animal-disease rules in some countries. Destination states and territories can add their own requirements.

U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) checks animals at the port of arrival. You must be able to show the required documents there.

Two paths: your own adopted pet, or a rescue importing dogs

This distinction matters. The CDC rules apply in both cases. The USDA Animal Welfare Act (AWA) commercial-import rules do not.

Individual adopter bringing their own adopted pet. If a dog is purchased or adopted from a foreign source and delivered to the new owners who will not resell the dog, USDA says the AWA live-dog import rules do not apply. The same is true for privately owned pet, service, or working dogs traveling with or being delivered to their owners. You still must meet CDC requirements, any USDA Veterinary Services disease rules that apply to the country of origin (for example foot-and-mouth disease or screwworm), CBP arrival checks, and the rules of your destination state or territory.

Source: USDA APHIS, Commercial Dog Import (“Dog Imports Not Covered”).

Rescue organization importing dogs for adoption. The AWA regulates dogs imported into the United States for resale, “whether through retail or wholesale channels or fee-based adoption.” CBP uses the same wording: Animal Care has additional requirements if you are importing dogs for resale, “such as sales through retail or wholesale channels or adoption for a fee or donation.” In that situation you must meet CDC rules plus the USDA APHIS Animal Care permit, age, vaccination, examination, and certificate rules described below — and any extra USDA Veterinary Services rules for certain diseases.

Sources: USDA APHIS, Commercial Dog Import; CBP, Bringing Pets and Wildlife into the United States.

If you are not sure which path you are on, read both sections and confirm with USDA APHIS Animal Care (Live Dog Import Permit Team) and CDC before you book travel.

Bringing an adopted dog into the United States

CDC rules that apply to every dog

CDC is the primary U.S. authority for dog imports, including dogs entering for the first time and U.S.-origin dogs returning from a trip. USDA APHIS states this clearly on its pet-dog and commercial-dog pages.

As of the pages fetched in this session, every dog entering or returning to the United States must:

  1. Have a CDC Dog Import Form receipt — one form per dog.
  2. Appear healthy upon arrival.
  3. Be at least 6 months old at the time of entry or return.
  4. Have a microchip that can be detected with a universal scanner.

Dogs that have been in a high-risk country for dog rabies in the 6 months before arrival have additional document, vaccination, and (in some cases) port-of-entry requirements. An unvaccinated dog that has been in a high-risk country in those 6 months will not be allowed to enter.

You must also follow USDA rules and the rules of your U.S. destination. CDC says dogs imported for commercial (resale) purposes have additional USDA requirements.

Sources: CDC, Bringing a Dog into the U.S. (page date shown: 1 August 2024); CDC, Entry Requirements for Dogs from Dog-Rabies Free or Low-Risk Countries (22 July 2024); CDC, Frequently Asked Questions on Dog Importations (updated 27 August 2026); USDA APHIS, Bring a Pet Dog into the United States (last modified 1 April 2026).

CDC offers a Dog Importation Navigator on its “Bringing a Dog into the U.S.” page to help you see which requirements apply to your situation.

Low-risk or rabies-free countries, or high-risk countries

CDC publishes a list of countries and political units that are high risk for dog rabies. The list fetched in this session is dated 15 April 2026.

CDC’s rule is simple:

  • If a country or political unit is not listed, it is not considered high risk for importing dog rabies into the United States. CDC treats it as dog rabies-free or low-risk.
  • The extra high-risk requirements apply if the dog has been in any listed high-risk country or political unit during the past 6 months.
  • For dogs that have not been in a listed high-risk country during those 6 months, CDC strongly recommends rabies vaccination, but the additional high-risk entry requirements do not apply. Those dogs follow the low-risk requirements.

Source: CDC, High-Risk Countries for Dog Rabies (15 April 2026). Always recheck this list before travel. It can change.

If the dog has been only in dog rabies-free or low-risk countries for the 6 months before U.S. entry:

  • The CDC Dog Import Form receipt is the only CDC form required (starting 1 August 2024).
  • The dog must still appear healthy, be at least 6 months old, and have a universally readable microchip.
  • The dog may enter at any U.S. airport, seaport, or land border crossing (including pre-clearance ports).

Source: CDC, Entry Requirements for Dogs from Dog-Rabies Free or Low-Risk Countries.

If the dog has been in any high-risk country in those 6 months:

  • The dog must be vaccinated against rabies and must meet additional documentation requirements. Without a valid rabies vaccination, the dog cannot enter.
  • U.S.-vaccinated dogs need the CDC Dog Import Form receipt and a Certification of U.S.-issued Rabies Vaccination form (completed by a USDA-accredited veterinarian before the dog leaves the United States; it cannot be issued retroactively). A USDA-endorsed export health certificate issued no later than 31 July 2025 may be accepted instead if it meets CDC’s conditions; certificates issued after that date are not accepted for re-entry.
  • Foreign-vaccinated dogs need the CDC Dog Import Form receipt, a Certification of Foreign Rabies Vaccination and Microchip form endorsed by an official government veterinarian, a reservation at a CDC-registered animal care facility, arrival by air at the airport where that facility is located, a microchip implanted before the rabies vaccine, and either a valid rabies serology titer from a CDC-approved laboratory or a 28-day quarantine reservation. They may not enter at a land border crossing.
  • The port of entry must match the one listed on the CDC Dog Import Form receipt.

Sources: CDC, Bringing a Dog into the U.S.; CDC, Entry Requirements for U.S.-Vaccinated Dogs from High-Risk Countries (28 April 2026); CDC, Entry Requirements for Foreign-Vaccinated Dogs from High-Risk Countries (22 July 2024); CDC FAQs.

The CDC Dog Import Form

Complete the form online from CDC’s official instructions page. After you submit it, CDC emails you to confirm your email address. Once you confirm, you receive a receipt. You may print the receipt or show it on your phone.

Official form and instructions: CDC Dog Import Form and Instructions (28 April 2026).

Key points from that page and the low-risk entry page:

  • The form is required for each dog.
  • It is free.
  • It should be completed by the person importing the dog (the importer, owner, or shipper). Someone else may complete it on the importer’s behalf if the importer has difficulty completing it (for example because of a disability).
  • CDC’s form instructions also list a flight parent — “a person transporting one or more dogs for the purpose of rescue, resale, or adoption on behalf of another person or an organization” — as someone who may be entered as the person bringing the dog.
  • If the dog is traveling by air, you must show the receipt to the airline before boarding, and you must be able to show it to CBP on arrival.
  • For dogs that have been only in dog rabies-free or low-risk countries in the 6 months before entry, the receipt is valid for one dog to enter multiple times from the same country within six months of issuance. Complete a new form if you are bringing another dog, if the dog later departs from a different country, or after six months. The country of departure on the receipt should match the country the dog is leaving to come to the United States.
  • For dogs coming from high-risk countries, CDC states that the receipt is valid for one dog to enter one time. Complete another form for future entries or other dogs.
  • You can fill the form out on the day of travel; CDC recommends completing it a few days, or up to six months, before travel from a low-risk country.
  • On 5 February 2026, CDC updated the form web system (new look and receipt formatting). CDC states there were no changes to importation requirements. Receipts from the previous version are still accepted if the information is accurate and the receipt is not expired.

Microchip notes from CDC FAQs (updated 27 August 2026):

  • Any brand is acceptable if it can be detected with a universal scanner.
  • If the microchip number starts with a number other than 9, CDC says to check with your veterinarian that it is readable.
  • For a dog that has been in a high-risk country, if the microchip cannot be scanned on arrival, the dog may be denied entry and returned at the importer’s expense.

CDC does not require a general certificate of health for pets to enter the United States. A health certificate may still be required by some states or by the airline.

Sources: CDC Dog Import Form and Instructions; CDC, low-risk entry requirements; CDC FAQs; CDC, Bringing an Animal into the U.S. (22 August 2025).

USDA Veterinary Services rules that can apply even to a privately owned dog

For a privately owned pet dog, APHIS Veterinary Services has additional requirements only if the dog is coming from a country affected by foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) or screwworm.

If the country or region is not free of FMD:

  • The dog’s fur and bedding must be free of excessive dirt, hay, or straw.
  • The dog should be bathed as soon as it reaches its destination and kept separate from all livestock for 5 days after entering the United States.
  • USDA states that no documentation is required to meet the FMD requirements.

If the country or region is one where screwworm is known to exist:

  • The dog may enter only with a certificate signed by a full-time salaried veterinary official (official government veterinarian) of the region of origin stating that the dog has been inspected for screwworm within 5 days before shipment and is either free from screwworm, or was infested, held in quarantine, and treated until free before leaving the region.
  • APHIS does not have a required template; the certification can be a standalone document or part of an export health certificate.

Check the country on USDA’s official tools before you travel:

  • Dog Imports: Check Country Disease Status
  • Animal Health Status of Regions (last modified 17 November 2026)

Source: USDA APHIS, Bring a Pet Dog into the United States.

Dogs used in livestock handling that are imported from any part of the world except Canada, Mexico, and certain regions of Central America and the West Indies should have no evidence of tapeworm infection (USDA, same page). That rule is unlikely to apply to a typical companion-animal adoption, but it is an official USDA requirement for livestock-handling dogs.

Rescue organizations: USDA Animal Welfare Act rules for dogs imported for adoption

These rules are in addition to CDC’s rules. They apply when dogs are imported for resale, which USDA defines to include transfer through retail or wholesale channels or adoption for a fee or donation. It is the importer’s responsibility to meet all requirements before the dog enters the United States.

USDA APHIS Animal Care requires that dogs imported for this purpose must be:

  • At least 6 months (180 days) of age at the time of entry.
  • Examined by a licensed veterinarian in the country of export and found to be in good health and free of infectious disease, internal parasites, and external parasites.
  • Vaccinated for rabies by a licensed veterinarian at least 30 days before the date of entry, unless the dog was previously vaccinated for rabies after at least 84 days of age with no lapse in vaccination status (the 30-day wait does not apply in that case).

- The dog must be at least 84 days of age at the time of that rabies vaccination. - The vaccination must be current at arrival. Two- and three-year vaccinations are acceptable; if the expiration date is not available, a 1-year validity is applied.

  • Vaccinated for distemper, hepatitis, parvovirus, parainfluenza, and leptospirosis (DHLPP) within 12 months of entry.
  • Accompanied by a health and rabies vaccination certificate issued in English by a veterinarian with a valid license in the country of export. USDA recommends APHIS Form 7041. The certificate must include identifying information (breed, sex, age, color, and other identifiers such as name, microchip, or tattoo if applicable), vaccine name, manufacturer, lot number, expiration date and validity period, the veterinarian’s license number, and the veterinarian’s signature.
  • Accompanied by a valid import permit issued by APHIS Animal Care.

How to get the permit. Apply online through APHIS eFile. USDA says to allow a minimum of 5 business days for processing (Monday–Friday, excluding federal holidays). The day you submit the application and the date the dog arrives do not count toward those 5 days. Incomplete or incorrect applications cause delays.

Live Dog Import Permit Team: ac.dogimport.mailbox@usda.gov; phone 816-737-4223.

Limited exceptions to the age and/or vaccination requirements may be granted for dogs imported into Hawaii from the British Isles, Australia, Guam, or New Zealand; dogs imported for resale for research; and dogs imported for veterinary treatment and subsequent resale. USDA says to contact the Live Dog Import Team in those cases.

Additional USDA Veterinary Services rules for commercial or adoption imports may apply if the country of origin is not free of African swine fever (ASF), foot-and-mouth disease, or screwworm. USDA tells importers to check the official country table:

Countries Where African Swine Fever and Other Diseases Are Known To Exist (last modified 24 February 2026).

If a country is not free of ASF, USDA describes extra conditions for commercial sale or adoption shipments (crate and dog free of dirt and natural bedding; bedding disposed of at the U.S. post-entry point of concentration; an ISO-compliant microchip verified immediately before bathing; each dog bathed at that post-entry point within 2 calendar days of arrival, documented on the Veterinary Services Dog Import Record). FMD and screwworm conditions for commercial imports are described on the same commercial-import page.

[Uncertain — commercial country table]: The official commercial-import country table is an interactive search box. The country-by-country rows did not appear in the text extracted from that page in this session. Do not assume a country’s ASF/FMD/screwworm status for commercial dog imports from the general “Animal Health Status of Regions” list alone: that list itself says its African swine fever status list “does not apply to importation of dogs for commercial sale or adoption,” and directs readers to the commercial-import country table. Check that table directly before a rescue shipment.

Source: USDA APHIS, How To Bring Dogs into the United States for Commercial Sale or Adoption (last modified 2 June 2026).

Bringing an adopted cat into the United States

Federal cat rules are simpler than dog rules, but they are not “no rules,” and your destination may add more.

USDA APHIS Veterinary Services states that it does not have any animal health requirements related to bringing (importing) a pet cat into the United States from a foreign country. USDA still tells you to check CDC and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and to check your destination state or territory.

Source: USDA APHIS, Bring a Pet Cat (Domestic) into the United States (last modified 24 December 2025).

CDC requires that cats appear healthy upon arrival. Cats are subject to inspection at ports of entry and may be denied entry if they have evidence of a zoonotic disease. If a cat appears to be ill, further examination by a licensed veterinarian at the owner’s expense might be required before the cat is allowed to enter.

Cats are not required to have proof of rabies vaccination for importation into the United States. CDC recommends that all cats be vaccinated against rabies, and your U.S. destination may have additional requirements.

All cats arriving in the state of Hawaii and the territory of Guam, even from the U.S. mainland, are subject to locally imposed quarantine requirements.

Source: CDC, Bringing an Animal into the U.S. (22 August 2025), Cats section.

CBP also states that all dogs and cats imported into the United States must be healthy upon arrival, and that a sick-looking animal may need further examination at the owner’s expense.

Source: CBP, Bringing Pets and Wildlife into the United States (last modified 17 November 2026).

If the animal is a wild species or hybrid rather than a domestic cat or dog, CBP notes that some wild species imported as pets may be protected under CITES or other wildlife laws. Check U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service before travel in that situation. This draft does not review wild-cat or hybrid import rules.

USDA’s commercial AWA live-dog import rules described above are written for dogs. This draft does not state a parallel AWA “cats for resale” permit scheme, because the official pages fetched here address commercial dog import.

State and territory rules (they can add requirements)

Federal entry is not the last word. CDC, USDA, and CBP all say you must also meet the rules of your U.S. destination.

  • CDC does not require a general health certificate for pets to enter the United States, but health certificates may be required for entry into some states, or by airlines. Check the state of final destination and the airline before you travel.
  • CDC also says to check your state or territory for laws on the sale, distribution, or transportation of animals.
  • USDA APHIS does not regulate the interstate movement of pets by their owners. Domestic movement requirements are set by the receiving state or territory. Those requirements may include a health certificate, updated vaccinations, diagnostic testing, or treatments.
  • USDA does regulate businesses that transport pets on behalf of pet owners.
  • For live animals entering a state or territory after an international import, USDA again says entry requirements are written and maintained by each receiving state or territory.

Find the destination agriculture department through the NASDA State Directory, and contact the state or territorial veterinarian if anything is unclear.

USDA also maintains a hub: Take a Pet From One U.S. State or Territory to Another (last modified 10 June 2026) and U.S. State and Territory Animal Entry Requirements.

Hawaii and Guam have extra, locally imposed rules. CDC states this clearly for cats, including cats coming from the U.S. mainland. [Uncertain for dogs at the federal-page level]: the CDC dog-import pages fetched in this session do not repeat the same Hawaii/Guam sentence in the extracts reviewed here. Do not assume the cat wording is the full rule for dogs. Check the official Hawaii and Guam animal-quarantine authorities before any movement of a dog or cat to those destinations — including a domestic move from the mainland.

CBP recommends contacting the anticipated port of arrival before importing a pet, and notes that pets excluded from entry will be returned at the owner’s expense.

Korea to USA adoption corridor

This subsection is about South Korea (the Republic of Korea). Do not confuse it with North Korea.

Rabies risk: South Korea is not a CDC high-risk country

On the official CDC High-Risk Countries for Dog Rabies list dated 15 April 2026, North Korea is listed. South Korea is not listed. There is no entry for “Korea,” “South Korea,” or “Republic of Korea.”

CDC states: if a country or political unit is not listed, it is not considered high risk for importing dog rabies into the United States, and dogs that have not been in any listed high-risk country during the past 6 months can meet the requirements for dogs from dog rabies-free or low-risk countries. CDC still strongly recommends rabies vaccination.

Source: CDC, High-Risk Countries for Dog Rabies (15 April 2026). Recheck the list before every trip.

What that means in practice, if the dog has been only in South Korea and other low-risk or rabies-free countries for the 6 months before U.S. entry:

  • Complete a CDC Dog Import Form for that dog and keep the receipt (show it to the airline and to CBP).
  • The dog must appear healthy, be at least 6 months old, and have a universally readable microchip.
  • The dog may arrive at any U.S. airport, seaport, or land border crossing.
  • CDC does not require a rabies certificate or a CDC-registered animal care facility reservation for this low-risk path — those extra high-risk steps apply only if the dog has been in a listed high-risk country in the previous 6 months.
  • If the dog visited a high-risk country in those 6 months (for example, a stay in a listed country before coming to Korea, or a trip from Korea to a listed country), the high-risk path applies, even if the last departure is from South Korea. The 6-month history is what matters.

This low-risk CDC path is the same whether you are an individual adopter or a rescue. A rescue importing dogs for adoption for a fee or donation must also meet the USDA AWA rules in the rescue section above.

USDA animal-disease status for South Korea (separate from rabies)

Rabies is a CDC issue. USDA also looks at other animal diseases.

On APHIS’s Animal Health Status of Regions list (last modified 17 November 2026):

  • Screwworm: South Korea does not appear on APHIS’s screwworm-affected regions list in the same document. Recheck before travel.
  • Check current USDA APHIS requirements before travel. See USDA APHIS, Pet Travel.

What this corridor does not cover

Korean export requirements (health certificate, vaccinations) also apply: check with Korea's Animal and Plant Quarantine Agency (APQA) before travel.

Destination U.S. state rules still apply after arrival, including any health certificate or vaccination the state requires, and the special Hawaii/Guam rules noted above.

Domestic adoption basics (a dog or cat already in the United States)

If you adopt a dog or cat that is already in the United States, CDC’s international dog-import form and the USDA live-dog import permit do not apply to that adoption itself. What does apply is mainly state, territorial, and local law, plus any rules of the shelter or rescue and, if you fly, the airline.

Official points:

  • Moving the animal to another state or territory is regulated by the destination state or territory, not by APHIS as an owner-led interstate pet move. The destination may require a health certificate (certificate of veterinary inspection), current vaccinations, tests, or treatments. Ask your veterinarian early, and confirm with the destination state veterinarian or agriculture department. Start here: USDA APHIS, Take a Pet From One U.S. State or Territory to Another and the NASDA State Directory.
  • Hawaii and Guam have locally imposed quarantine requirements for cats even when the cat comes from the U.S. mainland (CDC). Confirm the current official rules for both dogs and cats with Hawaii and Guam authorities before you travel.
  • Airlines often require a health certificate and may charge fees or restrict certain animals. CDC and CBP both say to check with the airline before the travel date.
  • Sale, distribution, or transportation of animals may also be regulated by your state or territory (CDC). If a U.S. rescue is transporting animals as a business, USDA notes that APHIS does regulate businesses that transport pets on behalf of pet owners.

This section is only the official movement-and-health baseline. It is not a guide to choosing a shelter, signing an adoption contract, or paying an adoption fee.

Checklist

Use this as a planning list, then confirm each item on the official pages. It is written first for an individual adopter bringing their own dog from a low-risk or rabies-free country (including South Korea, as long as the dog has not been in a high-risk country in the previous 6 months).

For every adopted dog entering the United States

  • Confirm, on the official CDC high-risk list, every country the dog has been in during the past 6 months.
  • Make sure the dog will be at least 6 months old on the day of U.S. entry.
  • Confirm the dog has a microchip readable by a universal scanner.
  • Complete a CDC Dog Import Form for this dog and keep the receipt (phone or print). Show it to the airline if flying, and to CBP on arrival.
  • Plan for the dog to appear healthy at arrival. If there is a medical issue, CDC recommends bringing medical records.
  • Ask the airline about its own health-certificate and booking rules.
  • Check USDA country disease status for FMD and screwworm (and follow those conditions if they apply).
  • Check the destination state or territory (and Hawaii or Guam, if relevant) for any extra health certificate, vaccination, or quarantine rule.
  • If the 6-month history includes a high-risk country, stop and follow the official high-risk path (U.S.-vaccinated or foreign-vaccinated) instead of this shorter list.

Extra steps if a rescue is importing dogs for adoption (fee or donation)

  • Meet every CDC item above.
  • Confirm the dog is at least 6 months (180 days) old at entry.
  • Arrange the export-country veterinary exam and the English health and rabies certificate (USDA recommends Form 7041).
  • Confirm current rabies vaccination (timing and the 30-day wait, or the no-lapse exception) and DHLPP within 12 months.
  • Apply for an APHIS Animal Care live-dog import permit in APHIS eFile and allow at least 5 business days.
  • Check the official commercial dog-import disease table for ASF, FMD, and screwworm, and follow any extra conditions.

For an adopted cat entering the United States

  • Plan for the cat to appear healthy at arrival (CDC inspection; further exam possible at your expense if the cat looks ill).
  • Rabies vaccination proof is not required by CDC for U.S. entry, but CDC recommends vaccination; check the destination state.
  • Check USDA/FWS only if the animal may not be a domestic cat (wild species or hybrid).
  • Check the destination state, the airline, and Hawaii or Guam rules if those are the destination (quarantine can apply even from the U.S. mainland).

For a domestic adoption and a move to another state

  • Confirm the destination state’s animal-entry rules with the state veterinarian or agriculture department.
  • Ask your veterinarian about any required health certificate, vaccinations, tests, or treatments.
  • If traveling by air, confirm the airline’s rules.
  • If the destination is Hawaii or Guam, read the official local quarantine rules before you book.
